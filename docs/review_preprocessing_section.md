The groundwater data come from a published, quality-controlled CGWB dataset [1], [2].

Rainfall comes from CHIRPS (Climate Hazards Center InfraRed Precipitation with Stations) [3]–[5].
CHIRPS blends satellite infrared rainfall estimates with rain-gauge observations into a
quasi-global, gridded record at 0.05° (about 5.5 km). We use version 3.0 monthly totals. We chose it
for three reasons. Its resolution is fine enough to give each well its own local value. Its record
starts before 2000, so rainfall windows are available for the very first groundwater rounds. And it
is openly downloadable as one file per month, which suits a sequential, resumable pipeline.

## Preprocessing: Big Data Analysis and its steps

Big data analysis is the process of acquiring, integrating, cleaning, transforming and validating
data that are too large, too fast-arriving, too varied or too unreliable for ad-hoc handling, so
that they can support trustworthy decisions. Its emphasis is on pipelines that are scalable,
reproducible and auditable, rather than on one-off scripts.

Our project fits the usual characteristics as follows. We describe them honestly, because the
groundwater table alone is not large.

**Volume.** The groundwater table is moderate: 2,759 wells × 92 rounds = 253,828 well-round rows
(about 59 MB as partitioned Parquet with all 84 columns). The volume comes from the rainfall side. We streamed 282
global CHIRPS rasters (plus 24 for 2023–24), each about 23 MB, which is roughly 7 GB of raw data. We
reduced it to 778,038 well-month values without ever holding more than one raster on disk.
Time-aware feature computation then evaluates expanding and rolling statistics, lags and windowed
sums for every well-round, which turns the table into 84 columns.

**Velocity.** The data arrive as a monthly stream of rasters. We process them sequentially with
resumable checkpoints, the same pattern an operational system would use for new months.

**Variety.** The project integrates a tabular, wide-format CSV of point measurements with gridded
GeoTIFF rasters. It also combines static attributes, such as aquifer class and well depth, with
time series.

**Veracity.** This is the dominant challenge. The original station identifiers are corrupted
(scientific-notation rounding), so we built a surrogate key. Missingness is uneven, with near-empty
rounds in May 2020/21, August 2012 and January 2016. A few readings are suspicious, and the
2023–24 data were less filtered.

**Value.** The result is a leakage-free, documented feature table and evaluation splits for
classifying seasonal groundwater stress and testing whether it generalises across regions.

The pipeline's steps, in order:

1. **Acquisition.** The groundwater dataset was used as delivered (it is not downloaded again). For
   rainfall, CHIRPS v3.0 monthly files were downloaded over HTTPS, one at a time, with polite retries.
   FTP was blocked in our environment.
2. **Ingestion.** We read the refined wide table without changing any value. Because only 2,411 of
   2,759 `Station Code` values were distinct, we assigned each well a surrogate `well_id`: a hash of
   state, district, station name and coordinates, unique for all 2,759 wells. A crosswalk keeps the
   original codes.
3. **Schema standardisation.** We reshaped the table from wide to long: one row per well and round,
   with the `Mon-YY` labels parsed into date, year, month and season. Empty rounds are kept, which
   gives 253,828 rows, of which 219,258 hold a reading (13.62% missing).
4. **Cleaning.** The dataset authors had already removed empty wells, wells with any negative value
   and 3σ outliers. They had also kept only wells with at least two readings every year and no value
   repeated more than twice in a row (Figure 2). We did not remove outliers again, because that would
   discard genuine extremes. Our cleaning converts placeholders to missing values (none were found),
   removes exact duplicates (none) and standardises names (6 states, 31 districts). It adds flags
   rather than deleting anything. The 11 zero readings are kept and flagged, and a composite
   `suspect_reading` flag marks 2,135 readings (0.97%) for sensitivity runs.
5. **Integration with rainfall.** Each well takes the nearest CHIRPS cell; nodata is treated as
   missing (none occurred). Because the day of each groundwater reading is unknown, rainfall features
   for a reading in month M use only months up to M−1. An assertion enforces this on every row.
6. **Transformation.** We computed rainfall totals over 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, the last completed
   Jun–Sep and Oct–Dec monsoons, and monsoon-year-to-date rainfall. Each total also has a deviation
   from the same well's earlier-years normal.
7. **Feature engineering.** All groundwater features use only earlier readings of the same well. They
   include previous readings, same-season lags, a Theil-Sen trend [6], [7], and a seasonal baseline
   with its anomaly score. The anomaly score has three variants: expanding (primary), rolling over the
   last 10 readings, and a small-sample t-adjusted version [8]. A stress class (Normal, Watch,
   Moderate Stress, High Stress) is assigned from the anomaly using thresholds of 1, 1.5 and 2.
   Perturb-the-future tests show that changing any future value changes no earlier feature.
8. **Reduction and filtering decisions.** No well was dropped: every well has at least 57 of 92
   readings. A baseline needs at least K = 5 earlier same-season readings, which leaves 24.2% of
   readings without one (Figure 11). For evaluation, seven near-empty main rounds are marked
   ineligible. In the extension, three rounds and 87 likely-placeholder zeros are also excluded.
9. **Validation.** We checked that coordinates fall inside India, that no rainfall cell is nodata,
   that rainfall totals are plausible, and that nearest-cell and 3×3-mean rainfall agree
   (r = 0.9991). A data contract fixes the type, range and missing-value policy of all 84 columns,
   and 62 automated tests enforce it.
10. **Storage.** The final table is stored as Parquet partitioned by state [14], so each state's data
    can be loaded directly for cross-region evaluation. A separate splits file defines chronological
    and state folds, and the 2023–24 extension is stored separately as a holdout. One command
    regenerates everything. A fresh clone reproduced all 18 outputs with zero differences.

## Results of preprocessing

**Figure 1** shows data availability by season and year. Most rounds cover over 90% of wells, but
May is low every year because 444 wells (all of Kerala and West Bengal, and most of Odisha and Assam)
have no May series. May 2020/21, August 2012 and January 2016 are almost empty. The 2023–24
extension, right of the divider, is far sparser.

**Figure 2 and Table 2** trace the cleaning stages. The authors' completeness rules reduce 32,299 raw
well rows to 2,759 reliable wells. Our own steps remove nothing and only add flags.

**Figure 3** shows that every well has 57–91 readings, so a total-readings cutoff was unnecessary.
**Figures 4 and 5** show uneven state coverage: Madhya Pradesh has the most wells (501) and Delhi the
fewest (9). Wells nonetheless span the country from Kerala to Punjab and Assam.

**Figure 6** shows the seasonal cycle: groundwater is deepest in May and shallowest in November, which
is why each reading is compared only with the same season.

**Figure 7** compares the anomaly scores. The raw score has heavy tails (from −56 to +39), so models
should use the capped score. **Figure 8** compares stress class counts under the three baselines.

**Figure 9** is a key methodological result. The expanding baseline labels 6.38% of readings High
Stress, against 2.28% expected for a normal distribution. The rolling baseline does not reduce this
(6.90%), so baseline lag is not the cause. The small-sample adjustment brings it to 4.18%, and a
Monte Carlo simulation confirms that a z-score from 5–10 readings exceeds 2 far more often than
nominal.

**Figure 10** shows how sensitive the label is to the thresholds (8.5–23.5% of classified readings
change), and **Figure 11** shows the cost of the minimum-history rule.

**Figure 12** shows that Tamil Nadu gets most of its rain in Oct–Dec, unlike the other states, which
is why Oct–Dec features were added. It also shows rainfall and groundwater depth for one example
well.

**Figure 13** illustrates the leakage controls for a single reading. **Figure 14** confirms that
nearest-cell and 3×3 rainfall agree closely. **Figure 15** gives the eligible rows per split and
season, and **Figure 16** quantifies how much weaker the 2023–24 extension coverage is (30.9%,
against 80.0% for 2018–22).

## Limitations

May readings are sparse throughout, and four states have none, so pre-monsoon stress cannot be
evaluated there. The near-empty rounds (August 2012, May 2020 and 2021, January 2016) leave gaps in
the labels. The authors kept only wells that were complete through 2022, so the dataset
over-represents well-maintained stations and the most recent rounds are slightly pre-selected for
quality. The 2023–24 extension has much weaker coverage and likely placeholder zeros, and it is
usable only as a secondary robustness check. Some recorded well depths appear outdated, so
"deeper than the well" flags mostly reflect metadata rather than bad readings. The day of each
reading is unknown, so rainfall can only be aligned to the month before it. Finally, even after the
small-sample adjustment, High Stress remains above its nominal rate. The excess is consistent with
drought years and with serial correlation, which the adjustment does not model.
